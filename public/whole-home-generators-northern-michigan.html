Whole-Home Standby Generators for Northern Michigan Winters

When lake-effect storms knock out the grid, a standby generator keeps your heat, water, and lights on — automatically. Here's what every homeowner should know.

Anyone who has spent a winter in Northern Michigan knows the drill: the wind picks up off the bay, the snow starts flying sideways, and somewhere out on the line a tree limb takes down the power. A short outage is an inconvenience. A long one — in single-digit temperatures — can quickly become a real emergency.

That's why more and more homeowners around Traverse City are installing whole-home standby generators. Unlike a portable unit you have to drag out and fuel in the middle of a storm, a standby generator kicks on automatically within seconds of an outage and runs your home until the power returns. In this guide, Matt Grace and the team at Grace Electric explain how they work, what size you need, and why professional installation makes all the difference.

Why Standby Power Matters Up North

Our region gets some of the heaviest snowfall in the country, and our tree-lined roads and rural service lines mean outages can last hours — sometimes days. A whole-home generator protects far more than your comfort:

  • Heat. Even gas furnaces need electricity to run their blowers and controls. No power means no heat — and frozen pipes.
  • Water. Homes on a well lose running water the moment the power goes out.
  • Frozen pipes. A prolonged winter outage can lead to burst pipes and thousands of dollars in water damage.
  • Food and medicine. Refrigerators, freezers, and medical equipment keep running without interruption.
  • Sump pumps. During a thaw or heavy rain, a working sump pump keeps your basement dry.
  • Peace of mind for seasonal homes. Cottage owners can protect their property even when they're not there.

How a Whole-Home Standby Generator Works

A standby generator is a permanently installed unit that sits outside your home, much like a central air conditioner. It's connected to a fuel source — typically natural gas or propane — and to an automatic transfer switch wired into your electrical panel. The process is completely hands-off:

  • The transfer switch constantly monitors your utility power.
  • When it detects an outage, it signals the generator to start — usually within seconds.
  • The transfer switch safely disconnects your home from the grid and connects it to generator power.
  • When utility power returns, it switches back automatically and shuts the generator down.

Because it runs on natural gas or a large propane tank, there's no refueling in the middle of a blizzard and no gasoline to store. And because the transfer switch isolates your home from the grid, there's no dangerous "backfeed" that could injure utility crews working to restore power.

What Size Generator Do You Need?

Generators are sized in kilowatts (kW), and the right size depends on what you want to power. You have two basic approaches:

  • Whole-home coverage. A larger unit (often 22kW or more) runs essentially everything — heat, water, kitchen, and even central air — just as if the grid were on.
  • Essential-circuits coverage. A smaller, more affordable unit powers the circuits that matter most: furnace, well pump, refrigerator, sump pump, and key lighting.

The best choice comes from an on-site load evaluation, not a guess. We'll look at your furnace, well, appliances, and priorities, then recommend a unit that fits both your home and your budget.

Standby vs. Portable Generators

Many homeowners start out with a portable generator, and they do have their place. But when it comes to reliable protection during a serious winter outage, a permanently installed standby unit is in a different league. Here's how they compare:

  • Automatic vs. manual. A standby unit starts on its own within seconds; a portable must be hauled out, fueled, and started — often in the dark and cold.
  • Fuel. Standby generators run on natural gas or a large propane tank, so there's no gasoline to store or refill mid-storm.
  • Capacity. A standby unit can power your whole home; portables typically run only a few items through extension cords.
  • Safety. A hardwired standby with a transfer switch eliminates the backfeed and carbon-monoxide risks that come with improvised portable hookups.
  • Convenience. With a standby generator, you may not even notice the power went out.

Keeping Your Generator Ready Year After Year

A standby generator is a long-term investment, and a little maintenance keeps it dependable for decades. Most units are designed to make this easy:

  • Automatic self-tests run the generator briefly on a schedule to confirm it's ready.
  • Annual service — oil, filters, and a full inspection — keeps it in peak condition.
  • Seasonal checks before winter ensure it will perform when it matters most.
  • Clear status indicators let you see at a glance that everything is working.

We're happy to walk you through a simple maintenance routine so your generator is always ready for the next storm.

Frequently Asked Questions

How quickly does a standby generator turn on?

Typically within seconds of an outage. The automatic transfer switch detects the loss of utility power, starts the generator, and switches your home over — often before you've even noticed the lights blinked.

Natural gas or propane — which is better?

If your home already has natural gas service, that's usually the most convenient choice since there's no tank to refill. Propane is an excellent option for homes without gas service and is very common in our more rural areas. We'll help you decide based on what's available at your property.

Is it noisy?

Modern standby generators are engineered to run quietly — far quieter than a portable unit — so they won't disturb your household or your neighbors during an outage.
